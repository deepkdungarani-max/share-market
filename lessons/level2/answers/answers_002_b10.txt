# Cum/ex-date, record date and corporate-action shortage handling

## Core idea
A buyer who purchases shares before the ex-date is entitled to the corporate benefit (dividend, bonus, rights). But if the seller fails to deliver and the shares reach the buyer after the record date, the buyer would miss the benefit. Exchanges handle this with special rules: shortages around record dates are auctioned in time if possible, otherwise **closed out directly** at a price that compensates the buyer, and the old "no delivery" period around corporate actions has been abolished for demat securities.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Cum and ex.** Trades up to the day before the ex-date are cum-benefit; under T+1, the ex-date equals the record date for most actions.
2. **Normal shortage process.** If a seller fails to deliver on T+1, NSE Clearing runs a buying-in auction after pay-out the same day, and delivers auction shares to the buyer.
3. **Record date timing.** A cum-benefit trade settled on the record date should give the buyer the benefit. If a shortage occurs and the auction pay-out cannot happen on or before the record date minus one business day, NSE's rules require the shortage to be closed out directly (no auction) at the highest price prevailing across exchanges over the relevant period (or as specified).
4. **Close-out value.** The close-out amount compensates the buyer for not receiving cum-benefit shares; it reflects the cum price including the benefit.
5. **No delivery period abolished.** Exchanges used to suspend deliveries around book closures; for compulsorily demat securities this "no delivery" period no longer exists.
6. **Broker-level internal shortages.** When buyer and seller are clients of the same broker, close-outs (since late 2025 executed by the CC at clearing member level) follow similar principles.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
A buyer purchases 200 shares cum-dividend at ₹1,000 the day before the record date; the dividend is ₹30. The seller fails to deliver.
- If the auction delivers shares before the record date: buyer gets shares and the ₹6,000 dividend.
- If not possible, the trade is closed out directly at, say, the highest price across exchanges in the window, ₹1,045: buyer receives 200 × 1,045 = ₹2,09,000, more than the ₹2,00,000 paid, which compensates for the missed ₹6,000 dividend and the missed shares.
- Seller's cost: 200 × (1,045 − 1,000) = ₹9,000 plus charges.

## India specifics
- Under T+1 (fully from January 2023), the ex-date and record date coincide for most actions, so shortages on the day before the record date are the critical case.
- Buyers whose shares are credited late due to short delivery do not get benefits directly from the company; brokers cannot claim them for the buyer later, which is why close-outs include compensation.
- Exchange circulars specify the treatment for each type of corporate action (dividend, bonus, rights, split).

## Real examples and history
- Investors sometimes complain of missing dividends or bonuses after buying just before the record date; in most cases the cause is a short delivery and subsequent close-out.
- The abolition of the no-delivery period simplified settlement for demat securities.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Buying on the record date.** Under T+1, a buy on the record date is ex-benefit; it is not a shortage issue.
- **BTST before a record date.** Selling shares bought the previous day can cause shortage chains near record dates.
- **Bonus and split shortages.** Close-out prices must reflect the corporate action ratio.
- **Assuming the company will pay a buyer who received shares late.** The company pays only holders on the record date.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Investors buying for dividends or bonuses buy at least one trading day before the record date and avoid relying on BTST sellers.
- Brokers track shortages around record dates and apply exchange close-out rules.
- Event-driven quants model the small risk of shortage around record dates.

## Related concepts
Record date and ex-date; T+1 settlement; shortage auctions; close-out pricing; BTST risk.

# BTST, T+1 delivery and early pay-in mechanics

## Core idea
BTST ("buy today, sell tomorrow") means selling shares on T+1 that were bought on T, before they are credited to the demat account. Under T+1 settlement this works because both trades settle on consecutive days, but it carries short-delivery risk if the original seller fails. **Early pay-in (EPI)** is the opposite situation: a seller delivers shares to the clearing corporation before settlement (usually on trade day), which removes the need for sale margins. SEBI made blocking mandatory for early pay-in in 2022 and moved in 2026 to give real-time margin benefits for accepted EPI.

## How it works, step by step
**BTST:**
1. Day T: buy 100 shares.
2. Day T+1: shares are paid out to the buyer through the settlement; the buyer can sell them on T+1 before receiving them in the demat account (shares are usually credited later on T+1).
3. Day T+2: the T+1 sale settles; the broker delivers the shares received on T+1.
4. Risk: if the T purchase faces a short delivery, the shares do not arrive on T+1, and the T+1 sale becomes a short delivery itself, leading to an auction or close-out cost.

**Early pay-in:**
1. A client selling shares from their holdings authorises delivery through eDIS (with TPIN and OTP), DDPI or other approved routes.
2. The shares are blocked in the client's demat account in favour of the clearing corporation (block mechanism mandatory since August 2022).
3. Once early pay-in is accepted, no margin is required on that sale (since delivery is assured).
4. SEBI revised EPI rules in 2026 so that an accepted early pay-in gives real-time margin relief on subsequent trades up to the accepted amount; reports give different effective dates (late 2026 and early 2027), so check the current circular.
5. BTST sales do not get EPI benefit, since the shares are not yet with the seller.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
A client sells ₹5 lakh of shares from holdings.
- Without EPI: the broker must collect margin (VaR + ELM, minimum 20% for stocks) = about ₹1 lakh blocked until settlement.
- With EPI via eDIS on trade day: no margin needed; ₹1 lakh stays free for other trades.
BTST risk: a client buys 1,000 shares at ₹200 on T and sells at ₹215 on T+1. If the T purchase is short-delivered and the auction for the client's T+1 sale fails, close-out could be at max(highest price, 1.2 × close); with close ₹212, close-out ≥ ₹254.4, costing about 1,000 × (254.4 − 215) = ₹39,400.

## India specifics
- India moved to T+1 in phases from 2022, completing in January 2023; payout timings were adjusted by SEBI's October 2024 circular.
- Optional T+0 settlement (since March 2024) applies to a limited set of stocks.
- Brokers may restrict BTST in stocks with high shortage risk (illiquid, surveillance-listed, near record dates).

## Real examples and history
- Under T+2, BTST meant selling shares two days before receipt, with a larger window for shortages; T+1 reduced that window.
- Early pay-in became important after peak-margin rules, as sellers sought to avoid blocking margin on sales of holdings.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **BTST in circuit stocks.** Shortage close-outs in stocks at upper circuit can be very expensive.
- **Not authorising eDIS.** Without authorisation, the sale cannot be delivered from holdings, causing shortage.
- **Reversing a sale after EPI.** Reversing the original sale revokes the EPI benefit.
- **Record dates.** BTST shortages around record dates may lead to direct close-outs.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Investors selling holdings authorise eDIS or DDPI to enable early pay-in and avoid margin blocks.
- Short-term traders weigh BTST convenience against shortage risk, especially in volatile small caps.
- Brokers' risk teams limit BTST in risky stocks.

## Related concepts
T+1 settlement; shortage auctions and close-out; peak margin; DDPI and eDIS; record dates.

# Trade-for-trade, Z-group and ASM/GSM segments: direct close-out and restrictions

## Core idea
Exchanges place risky or non-compliant stocks into special categories that restrict trading: the **trade-for-trade (T2T)** segment, where every trade must be settled by delivery (no intraday netting); BSE's **Z group** for companies failing listing compliance; and surveillance frameworks such as **ASM** (Additional Surveillance Measure) and **GSM** (Graded Surveillance Measure). These restrictions cut speculation and manipulation, but they also make these stocks less liquid and riskier to trade, including higher margins and harsher handling of short deliveries.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Trade-for-trade (T2T or BE series).** Each trade is settled individually by delivery; intraday squaring off is not allowed (buying and selling the same stock the same day creates two delivery obligations). Price bands are typically narrow (2% or 5%).
2. **Z group (BSE).** Companies that fail to comply with listing requirements (for example not filing results) or investor grievance rules are moved here; trading may be restricted to T2T and periodic call auctions.
3. **ASM.** Applied to stocks with unusual price or volume movements, concentrated trading or high volatility; stages escalate to 100% margins, T2T and narrower price bands.
4. **GSM.** For stocks whose price is not supported by fundamentals (for example shell-like companies); stages restrict trading frequency (for example once a week), require additional deposits from buyers, and limit price movement.
5. **Shortages.** In restricted segments, shortages may be handled with direct close-out rather than auctions, depending on exchange rules, because auctions in illiquid stocks often fail.
6. **Review.** Exchanges review lists periodically; stocks move between stages or exit when criteria are no longer met.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
A trader buys 1,000 shares of a T2T stock at ₹50 and sells them the same day at ₹51:
- Normal stock: the trades net off; profit ₹1,000 minus costs.
- T2T stock: the buy must be paid for and delivered (₹50,000); the sale must be delivered from existing holdings. If the trader had no holdings, the sale is a short delivery, leading to auction or close-out, possibly at a much higher price.

GSM example: a buyer in GSM stage II must deposit 100% of trade value as margin, plus an additional deposit (for example 50% of trade value) held for a period; trading allowed only on specified days.

## India specifics
- ASM and GSM frameworks were introduced in 2018 and 2017 respectively, with criteria and stages updated over time.
- Exchanges also place stocks in Enhanced Surveillance Measure (ESM) for SME and small companies.
- Lists are published daily; brokers block or restrict trading in these stocks.

## Real examples and history
- Many small companies with sharp, unexplained price rises during speculative phases (for example 2021 to 2024) were placed under ASM, after which their volumes and prices often fell.
- GSM was introduced after SEBI's 2017 action against suspected shell companies.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Intraday trading in T2T stocks.** Not allowed; leads to delivery obligations.
- **Surveillance is not a sell signal by itself.** But it signals elevated risk and reduced liquidity.
- **Margins can jump to 100%.** Leveraged positions may be squared off.
- **Exit difficulty.** Narrow bands and limited trading days can trap investors.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Investors check ASM, GSM and T2T lists before buying small caps.
- Quants exclude or flag restricted stocks in backtests, since their liquidity and trading rules differ.
- Brokers configure risk systems to block intraday and leveraged trades in these stocks.

## Related concepts
Surveillance measures; circuit limits; shortages and close-outs; market manipulation; SME stocks.

# Modelling auction and close-out loss as an option-like payoff

## Core idea
A seller who fails to deliver shares faces an uncertain cost: if the exchange's buying-in auction succeeds, they pay the auction price minus their sale price (but never gain if the auction is cheaper); if the auction fails, they pay a close-out price that is the higher of the highest price over a window and a 20% markup on the auction-day close. Both payoffs look like options the seller has written: a call on the price and a lookback with a floor. Modelling them this way shows that short delivery costs much more on average than the typical daily price move suggests.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Payoff if the auction succeeds.** Cost = max(P_auction − P_sale, 0), since a cheaper auction price does not benefit the defaulting seller (the difference does not flow back to them). This is a call option written by the seller with strike P_sale on the auction price.
2. **Payoff if the auction fails.** Cost = max(max(H, 1.2 × C) − P_sale, 0), where H is the highest traded price from trade day to auction day and C the auction-day close. This combines a lookback call (on H) with a floor at 20% above C.
3. **Probability of auction failure.** Depends on liquidity and price behaviour: higher for illiquid, circuit-hitting or restricted stocks.
4. **Expected cost** = (1 − p_fail) × E[auction cost] + p_fail × E[close-out cost].
5. **Simulation.** Simulate price paths (for example lognormal with daily volatility), compute intraday highs, apply the rules, and average.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
Simulation (200,000 paths) for a stock sold at ₹400 with 2.5% daily volatility, one day to the auction, auction price 0.5% above the next close, and a 20% chance that the auction fails:
- Expected auction cost: about ₹5.1 per share (1.3%).
- Expected close-out cost: about ₹80 per share (20%), and never less than about ₹31 in the simulation, because the 1.2 × close floor dominates.
- Blended expected cost: about ₹20 per share (5% of price); 99th percentile about ₹100 per share.
So a short delivery on 1,000 shares has an expected cost near ₹20,000 and a tail cost near ₹1 lakh, far more than a typical one-day move of ₹10 per share.

## India specifics
- NSE Clearing's close-out rule (higher of the highest price from trade day to auction day or 20% above the auction-day close) creates the floor that makes failed auctions expensive.
- Since late 2025 the CC executes close-outs for internal shortages too, so brokers' internal policies converge with exchange rules.
- Near record dates and in restricted stocks, direct close-outs (without auction) increase p_fail effectively to 1.

## Real examples and history
- Short sellers in upper-circuit stocks who could not deliver have faced close-out costs far above the day's move, because auctions found no sellers.
- Option-style analysis of penalty rules is common in market design: penalties are set so the expected cost of failing exceeds any benefit from deliberate short delivery.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Using only expected auction cost.** Ignores the fat tail from failed auctions.
- **Ignoring path dependence.** The highest price over the window matters, not just the close.
- **Correlation between price jumps and auction failure.** Auctions are more likely to fail when prices spike, making losses worse than an independence assumption suggests.
- **Costs and penalties.** Exchanges and brokers may add charges on top.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Risk teams estimate expected and tail costs of BTST and short-delivery risk by stock type and set restrictions.
- Market designers evaluate whether penalties deter deliberate failures.
- Traders understand why naked short selling in Indian cash markets is costly.

## Related concepts
Shortage auctions and close-outs; lookback options; Monte Carlo simulation; BTST; circuit limits; securities lending.

# Layer-by-layer reading of SEBI CIR/MRD/DRMNP/25/2014 and the Master Circular for clearing corporations

## Core idea
SEBI's circular of 27 August 2014 (CIR/MRD/DRMNP/25/2014) is the foundation of how Indian clearing corporations protect markets against member defaults. It created the **Core Settlement Guarantee Fund**, standardised the **default waterfall**, and required **stress testing** to size the fund. Later circulars revised parts of it, and SEBI consolidates such rules in master circulars for stock exchanges and clearing corporations. Reading it layer by layer explains today's risk architecture.

## How it works, step by step (the layers)
1. **Objective.** Create a fund within the SGF against which no exposure is given, available readily and unconditionally to meet settlement obligations if a member defaults.
2. **Segment-wise Core SGF.** Each CC maintains a Core SGF for each segment (equity cash, equity derivatives, currency, and so on), so losses in one segment do not drain another.
3. **Minimum Required Corpus (MRC).** Determined by stress testing, with the corpus fixed for a month and reviewed monthly; daily stress tests monitor adequacy.
4. **Contributions.** CC at least 50% of MRC; stock exchange at least 25%; clearing members up to 25% (members' contributions risk-based and not exceeding the CC's contribution); penalties and fines added; investment of the fund in safe, liquid assets.
5. **Stress testing.** Daily credit stress tests using historical and hypothetical scenarios to estimate losses from the default of the largest members; liquidity stress tests; reverse stress tests; periodic review of scenarios. Methods were revised later (for example equity derivatives in October 2024).
6. **Default waterfall.** Defaulter's monies and collateral → insurance (if any) → CC resources up to a specified share ("skin in the game") → Core SGF in order (penalties, CC contribution, exchange contribution, non-defaulting members' contributions pro rata) → remaining CC resources (to the extent specified) → capped contributions from non-defaulting members → pro-rata loss allocation as a last resort.
7. **Replenishment.** After use, the fund must be replenished promptly, with rules on who contributes.
8. **Disclosure.** CCs publish Core SGF sizes, contributions and stress-testing summaries.
9. **Master circulars.** SEBI periodically issues master circulars for stock exchanges and clearing corporations that consolidate the 2014 circular and later amendments; always read the latest consolidated version.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
Equity derivatives segment MRC from stress tests: ₹5,000 crore.
- CC contribution ≥ ₹2,500 crore; exchange ≥ ₹1,250 crore; members ≤ ₹1,250 crore (in practice often less, with CC and exchange covering more).
- Cash segment MRC: ₹800 crore, held separately.
A derivatives default leaving a ₹600 crore loss after the defaulter's margins: CC skin in the game (say 5% of MRC = ₹250 crore) first, then ₹350 crore from the derivatives Core SGF (starting with penalties and CC contribution). The cash segment fund is untouched. The CC replenishes the derivatives fund.

## India specifics
- The 2014 framework applied across recognised CCs (NSE Clearing, ICCL, MCX Clearing and others) and was extended with adaptations to commodity derivatives CCs and the limited-purpose CC for corporate bond repo.
- In 2016 SEBI directed refunds of members' earlier SGF contributions in line with the framework's emphasis on CC and exchange funding.
- Revisions include the October 2024 equity derivatives stress-testing methodology and changes to commodity stress tests.

## Real examples and history
- The circular aligned India with the CPMI-IOSCO Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures (2012), particularly on credit risk, default management and stress testing.
- No major Indian CC default has required the Core SGF to be used in a way that hurt non-defaulting members, but the framework is designed for that possibility.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Treating SGF as one pool.** Core SGF is segment-wise.
- **Confusing MRC with total resources.** Margins and CC capital are separate layers.
- **Reading only the 2014 text.** Later circulars and master circulars modify it.
- **Member contributions.** Capped and risk-based; members cannot be required to contribute more than the CC.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Clearing members understand their potential exposure in the waterfall.
- Analysts of exchange groups track Core SGF contributions as capital commitments.
- Students and risk professionals use the circular as a template for CCP risk design.

## Related concepts
Clearing corporations and novation; stress testing; default waterfall; CCP recovery tools; PFMI; segment-wise risk management.

<qa difficulty="medium"><q>What is the Core SGF, according to SEBI's 2014 circular?</q><a>A fund within the settlement guarantee fund of each clearing corporation, maintained segment-wise, against which no exposure is given, so it is readily and unconditionally available to meet settlement obligations if a clearing member defaults.</a></qa>
<qa difficulty="hard"><q>Why is the Core SGF maintained segment-wise?</q><a>So that losses from a default in one segment (for example equity derivatives) do not drain resources protecting another segment (for example cash). Each segment's risk is covered by its own stress-tested fund, limiting contagion across segments.</a></qa>
<qa difficulty="hard"><q>For an MRC of ₹1,600 crore, what are the minimum CC and exchange contributions and the maximum member contribution?</q><a>CC at least 50% = ₹800 crore; exchange at least 25% = ₹400 crore; members at most 25% = ₹400 crore (and not more than the CC's contribution).</a></qa>
<qa difficulty="expert"><q>List the default waterfall order under the 2014 framework.</q><a>Defaulter's monies and collateral; insurance, if any; CC's own resources up to a specified amount (skin in the game); Core SGF in order (penalties, CC contribution, exchange contribution, non-defaulting members' primary contributions pro rata); remaining CC resources to the extent specified; capped additional contributions from non-defaulting members; and finally pro-rata allocation of any remaining loss.</a></qa>
<qa difficulty="medium"><q>How often is the MRC reviewed?</q><a>The MRC is determined from stress tests and reviewed monthly (with the fund replenished to the required level), while stress tests run daily to monitor adequacy.</a></qa>
<qa difficulty="hard"><q>Why should students read the latest master circular rather than only the 2014 circular?</q><a>SEBI has amended the framework many times (stress-testing methods, contributions, segments such as commodities and the LPCC), and master circulars consolidate these changes. Relying only on the 2014 text would miss current requirements.</a></qa>
