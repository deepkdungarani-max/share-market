# Schemes of arrangement (ss.230–232), delisting, and squeeze-out valuation for minority shareholders

## Core idea
Controlling shareholders often want to merge companies, split them, take them private or buy out minorities. Indian law offers several routes: schemes of arrangement approved by shareholders, creditors and the NCLT (sections 230 to 232 of the Companies Act 2013); voluntary delisting under SEBI's Delisting Regulations 2021; and squeeze-outs of small minorities after reaching 90% (section 236). In each route, the key minority protection is price: valuation reports, fairness opinions, price-discovery mechanisms and, for listed companies, votes in which public shareholders' views decide.

## How it works, step by step
**Schemes of arrangement (sections 230 to 232):**
1. Board approves a scheme (merger, demerger, capital restructuring) with a share-exchange ratio based on a registered valuer's report and a fairness opinion.
2. For listed companies, the scheme goes to the stock exchanges and SEBI for an observation letter before filing with the NCLT.
3. Meetings of shareholders and creditors: approval needs a majority in number representing three-fourths in value of those voting. For certain listed-company schemes (for example involving promoters or related parties), SEBI requires that votes of public shareholders in favour exceed votes against.
4. Objections can be raised only by holders of at least 10% of shares or 5% of debt.
5. NCLT sanctions the scheme, which then binds everyone.
6. Small and holding-subsidiary mergers can use the fast-track route (section 233).

**Voluntary delisting (SEBI Delisting Regulations 2021, amended 2024):**
1. Acquirer (usually the promoter) announces intent; board and shareholder approvals (public shareholders' votes in favour must be at least twice those against).
2. **Reverse book building (RBB):** public shareholders tender at prices they choose above a floor price; the discovered price is the one at which the acquirer's holding reaches 90%. The acquirer can accept, reject, or make a counter-offer within rules.
3. **Fixed price route (since September 2024):** for frequently traded shares, the acquirer can offer a fixed price at least 15% above the floor price; delisting succeeds if the 90% threshold is reached.
4. Remaining shareholders can tender for a period after delisting at the final price.

**Squeeze-out (section 236):** an acquirer holding 90% or more can offer to buy the remaining shares at a price determined by a registered valuer; minority holders can also require purchase.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
**RBB delisting.** Promoter holds 75% of 10 crore shares; floor price ₹400. Public bids (cumulative shares tendered at or below each price): 6 lakh at ₹420, 25 lakh up to ₹450, 1.6 crore up to ₹480, 2.2 crore up to ₹520. To reach 90%, the promoter needs 1.5 crore shares from the public (15%). The discovered price is the lowest at which cumulative tenders reach 1.5 crore: ₹480.

**Fixed price alternative.** With a floor of ₹400, the fixed price must be at least ₹460 (15% premium). If public holders tender 1.5 crore shares at ₹460, delisting succeeds at a lower price than RBB, but if they do not, it fails.

**Merger swap ratio.** Target value per share ₹300, acquirer ₹1,200: ratio = 1 acquirer share for 4 target shares.

## India specifics
- The 2024 amendments added the fixed-price route, changed the floor price computation and adjusted counter-offer rules, after many RBB delistings failed or ended with prices far above expectations because a few shareholders could hold out.
- Vedanta's 2020 delisting attempt failed under RBB when tendered shares fell short, one of the episodes that drove reforms.
- Holding-subsidiary mergers (for example HDFC Ltd into HDFC Bank in 2023) used schemes approved by both companies' shareholders and the NCLT.

## Real examples and history
- Schemes have been used both to restructure groups efficiently and, at times, to transfer value from minorities; SEBI's observation letters and public-shareholder voting rules aim to check this.
- Selective capital reductions under section 66 have also been used to squeeze out minorities in unlisted companies, with court-approved fair value.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Swap ratio fairness.** Minority holders should check valuation methods and comparable ratios.
- **Hold-out strategies in RBB.** Some investors bid very high, hoping the promoter accepts; the counter-offer and fixed-price routes reduce this.
- **Tax.** Shares received in an amalgamation are generally tax-neutral; cash received in a delisting is taxable as capital gains.
- **Residual shareholders.** After delisting, illiquid shares remain; the exit window matters.
- **Deemed approvals.** Count public shareholder votes separately where SEBI requires it.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Special-situation investors buy stocks with likely delistings and estimate discovered prices from shareholding patterns and past cases.
- Minority investors vote on schemes and delisting proposals, comparing offers with intrinsic value.
- Analysts compute swap-ratio arbitrage between merging listed companies.

## Related concepts
Open offers (SAST); minimum public shareholding; section 66 capital reduction; registered valuers; fairness opinions; public shareholder voting.

# Governance risk scoring: promoter pledge, RPT intensity, auditor red flags and proxy-advisor signals

## Core idea
Many Indian stock disasters were governance failures before they were business failures: promoters pledging most of their shares, money moved through related parties, auditors resigning, and boards ignoring red flags. A governance risk score combines observable signals into a single number that helps investors avoid or size down risky companies. It does not predict fraud with certainty, but stocks with many red flags have historically been far more likely to suffer sudden collapses.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Choose signals** that are public and measurable:
   - **Promoter pledge:** pledged shares as a share of promoter holding (from SAST Regulation 31 disclosures and shareholding patterns).
   - **RPT intensity:** related-party transactions as a share of revenue or assets; size of loans to related parties.
   - **Auditor red flags:** auditor resignations (with reasons, required since SEBI's October 2019 circular), qualified opinions, emphasis of matter, frequent auditor changes, small audit firms for large companies.
   - **Board signals:** independent director resignations, low board independence, long-tenured IDs.
   - **Proxy-advisor signals:** negative recommendations from proxy advisers (IiAS, InGovern, SES) and failed or close votes.
   - **Regulatory signals:** SEBI orders, exchange SOP fines, surveillance measures.
2. **Score each signal** (for example 0 to 3) using thresholds.
3. **Weight and combine** into a total score; test weights on historical data.
4. **Validate.** Check whether high scores preceded large drawdowns, defaults or regulatory actions, using point-in-time data.
5. **Use.** Exclude, underweight or demand a higher return for high-score stocks.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
Scoring (0 = fine, 3 = severe):
- Pledge: under 10% = 0; 10 to 30% = 1; 30 to 60% = 2; above 60% = 3.
- RPT intensity (RPT/revenue): under 5% = 0; 5 to 15% = 1; 15 to 30% = 2; above 30% = 3.
- Auditor: no issues = 0; emphasis of matter = 1; qualified opinion = 2; mid-term resignation = 3.
- ID resignation citing governance concerns in last year: yes = 3.
- Proxy-advisor "against" on key resolutions in last year: 2.
Company A: pledge 45% (2), RPT 18% (2), auditor resigned mid-term (3), ID resignation (3), proxy advice against (2): total 12 out of 14 → very high risk.
Company B: pledge 5% (0), RPT 3% (0), clean audit (0), no resignations (0), proxy concerns on pay (2): total 2 → low risk.

## India specifics
- SEBI's October 2019 circular requires auditors who resign to complete the limited review or audit for the quarter (and in some cases the next quarter, or the full year) before leaving, and companies must disclose the auditor's reasons within 24 hours; the audit committee must deliberate on the concerns.
- The circular followed a wave of abrupt auditor resignations in 2018 to 2019 at companies that later faced serious trouble.
- Proxy advisers are regulated by SEBI (under its research analyst regulations, with procedural guidelines since 2020).
- Related-party rules (LODR Regulation 23, revised in 2025) and pledge disclosures provide the raw data.

## Real examples and history
- Cases such as the Satyam fraud (2009), the Essel group's pledge crisis (2019) and many smaller companies showed combinations of high pledging, opaque related-party dealings and auditor changes before collapse.
- Global studies find that governance scores have weak links to average returns but stronger links to downside risk.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Signals can be benign.** Pledges for business expansion or auditor rotation can look like red flags.
- **Look-ahead bias.** Use only information disclosed at the time; restated data and later news must not leak in.
- **Over-weighting one signal.** Combine signals; single flags create many false alarms.
- **Ignoring sector norms.** RPT intensity is naturally higher in group structures with captive suppliers.
- **Static scores.** Update after each disclosure; deterioration matters more than the level.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Fund managers use governance screens to exclude high-risk stocks before fundamental analysis.
- Quants add governance scores as risk factors or constraints in portfolio optimisation.
- Credit analysts use the same signals for lending decisions to promoter groups.

## Related concepts
Promoter pledging; related-party transactions; auditor independence; independent directors; proxy advisers; SEBI SOP fines; surveillance measures.

# Stress testing methodologies for MRC: stressed VaR, filtered historical simulation and factor models, with back-testing

## Core idea
A clearing corporation's Core Settlement Guarantee Fund must be large enough to cover the losses from the default of its largest members in extreme but plausible market moves. The size, the minimum required corpus (MRC), comes from daily stress tests. Choosing the stress scenarios is the hard part: purely historical scenarios may miss new risks; purely hypothetical ones may be arbitrary. Clearing corporations combine methods such as stressed value-at-risk, filtered historical simulation and factor-based scenarios, and check them through back-testing. SEBI revised the equity derivatives stress-testing framework in October 2024.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Positions.** Take every clearing member's (and its clients') positions at end of day.
2. **Scenarios.** Generate price moves for all underlyings:
   - **Historical:** the worst observed moves over a long period (for example the 2008 crisis, March 2020), applied to current positions.
   - **Stressed VaR:** VaR computed from a stressed window of data (a period of high volatility), at high confidence, over a multi-day period of risk (the time needed to close out a defaulter, for example 2 to 3 days).
   - **Filtered historical simulation (FHS):** take standardised historical returns (returns divided by their volatility at the time), then rescale by current or stressed volatility. This keeps fat tails and correlations from history while reflecting today's volatility.
   - **Factor models:** shock a few factors (index level, sector moves, volatility) and map them to each position, allowing hypothetical scenarios (index −15%, IV +50%).
3. **Losses.** For each member, loss = position value change minus its margins and collateral.
4. **Cover requirement.** MRC is typically set by the loss from the simultaneous default of the members with the largest stressed exposures (a "cover 2" or similar standard), taking the worst scenario.
5. **Back-testing.** Compare actual daily P&L of members' portfolios with margins and stress estimates; count breaches; investigate and recalibrate if breaches exceed expectations.
6. **Governance.** Methods and results are reviewed by risk committees and reported to SEBI.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
**FHS.** Historical day: Nifty fell 5% when its volatility was 40% annualised. Standardised shock = −5%/40% = −0.125. Today's volatility is 14%; stressed volatility assumption 28%. Scenario move = −0.125 × 28% = −3.5% for one day; over a 3-day period of risk (√3 scaling) ≈ −6.1%.

**Member loss.** Member X is net short index puts with a delta-equivalent exposure of ₹4,000 crore and short gamma; a −6.1% move causes a modelled loss of ₹420 crore (₹244 crore from delta, ₹176 crore from gamma and vega). X's margins are ₹300 crore, so the uncovered loss is ₹120 crore. Member Y's uncovered loss is ₹90 crore. If MRC is based on the two largest uncovered losses, MRC = ₹210 crore from this scenario; the final MRC is the maximum across scenarios.

**Back-testing.** At 99% confidence, about 2.5 breaches a year (250 days) are expected; 10 breaches suggests the model underestimates risk.

## India specifics
- SEBI's circular of 1 October 2024 (SEBI/HO/MRD/MRD-PoD-2/P/CIR/2024/131) revised the equity derivatives stress-testing framework used to set Core SGF MRC, introducing new methodologies applied uniformly across clearing corporations; summaries describe a stressed-VaR approach with a 3-day period of risk.
- The circular allowed a one-time transfer of surplus from the equity cash segment's Core SGF to meet higher derivatives MRC, with conditions.
- Commodity derivatives stress tests were revised separately (for example a change in the Z-score threshold in 2026).
- The original 2014 framework required daily stress tests with both historical and hypothetical scenarios.

## Real examples and history
- Global standards (CPMI-IOSCO's Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures) require CCPs handling systemic markets to cover at least the default of the two members creating the largest exposures in extreme but plausible conditions.
- After the 2018 Nasdaq Clearing default, regulators emphasised stress tests that capture concentrated, illiquid positions.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Correlation breakdowns.** In crises correlations rise; diversified-looking portfolios lose together.
- **Liquidity and concentration.** Closing out a large position takes longer and moves prices; add concentration add-ons.
- **Option non-linearity.** Use full revaluation, not delta approximations, for large moves.
- **Pro-cyclicality.** Raising margins sharply in a crisis can worsen it; floors and buffers smooth margins over time.
- **Overfitting scenarios** to recent history.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- CC risk teams run and validate these models daily.
- Large trading firms run similar stress tests on their own books to anticipate margin calls.
- Analysts of exchanges watch MRC changes, which tie up capital.

## Related concepts
Core SGF and default waterfall; SPAN and VaR margins; extreme loss margin; PFMI; back-testing; pro-cyclicality.

# CCP recovery and resolution tools: cash calls, variation-margin gains haircutting, tear-up and the PFMI/FSB resolution regime

## Core idea
A central counterparty (CCP) is designed so that a member's default is absorbed by the defaulter's margins and the default fund. But what if losses exceed all prefunded resources? A CCP that fails would threaten the whole market. So international standards require CCPs to have **recovery** tools (to restore themselves using predefined loss-sharing) and authorities to have **resolution** powers (to take over and keep critical functions running). These tools allocate losses to surviving members and their clients, and their fairness is hotly debated.

## How it works, step by step
**Prefunded waterfall (normal default management):** defaulter's margins and default fund contribution → CCP's own capital ("skin in the game") → non-defaulters' default fund contributions.

**Recovery tools (if losses go beyond prefunded resources):**
1. **Cash calls (assessments):** surviving members must contribute extra funds, usually capped (for example one or two times their default fund contribution).
2. **Variation margin gains haircutting (VMGH):** the CCP reduces the variation margin it pays to members with gains, using that money to cover losses; members with losses still pay in full.
3. **Partial tear-up:** the CCP terminates some contracts (for example those of the defaulted member's unbalanced book) at a set price, to rebalance its book when auctions fail.
4. **Full tear-up:** terminating all contracts in a product or service, effectively closing it.
5. **Forced allocation:** assigning the defaulter's positions to surviving members (controversial).
6. **Initial margin haircutting:** using non-defaulters' initial margin; widely opposed and generally not allowed.

**Resolution (by authorities):** if recovery fails or would harm stability, a resolution authority takes control, uses similar tools plus its own powers (transfers, bridge CCPs, write-downs of CCP equity), and must respect the "no creditor worse off than in liquidation" principle.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
After a default, a CCP still faces a ₹600 crore uncovered loss after all prefunded resources.
- **Cash call:** 40 surviving members, each with a capped assessment of 1× their default fund contribution, totalling ₹400 crore. Remaining loss ₹200 crore.
- **VMGH:** on the next settlement, members with gains are due ₹1,000 crore of variation margin. Haircut = 200/1,000 = 20%; they receive ₹800 crore. A member due ₹50 crore gets ₹40 crore, losing ₹10 crore although it did nothing wrong.
- If losses keep growing (auction failure), the CCP may partially tear up contracts to rebalance its book.

## India specifics
- SEBI's 2014 framework for Indian clearing corporations sets a standard default waterfall: defaulter's resources, insurance, CC's own contribution, Core SGF (with CC, exchange and member contributions), further CC resources, and then capped additional contributions from non-defaulting members, with remaining losses allocated pro rata (for example by haircuts on pay-outs) as a last resort.
- Indian CCs are also overseen by the RBI for certain segments (and the LPCC for corporate bond repo has RBI authorisation).
- India's legal framework for CCP resolution is less developed than the EU's dedicated regulation; resolution of market infrastructure would rely on SEBI's powers and specific legislation.

## Real examples and history
- CPMI-IOSCO published guidance on FMI recovery (2014, updated 2017); the FSB issued guidance on CCP resolution (2017) and later work on financial resources for resolution (2020 onward).
- The EU adopted a CCP Recovery and Resolution Regulation (2021), giving authorities powers including VMGH and tear-ups.
- Industry views differ: dealers' associations accept capped VMGH at the end of the waterfall but oppose initial margin haircutting and forced allocation; buy-side firms argue VMGH unfairly penalises "winners".

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Assuming CCPs cannot fail.** Very unlikely, but planning assumes they could.
- **Clients' exposure.** Clients of clearing members can be affected through VMGH if passed on.
- **Wrong-way risk.** Losses are largest in crises, when members are also stressed and cash calls are hardest to meet.
- **Incentives.** Caps on assessments limit members' exposure but shift losses elsewhere.
- **Legal certainty.** Tools must be in the CCP's rules beforehand to be enforceable.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Clearing members model their contingent exposure to assessments and VMGH in stress scenarios.
- Buy-side firms review CCP rulebooks to understand potential losses from recovery tools.
- Regulators run system-wide stress tests linking CCPs and their members.

## Related concepts
Default waterfall; Core SGF; stress testing; PFMI; FSB key attributes of resolution regimes; interoperability; wrong-way risk.

# Limited Purpose Clearing Corporation (ICCL's LPCC) Core SGF design for repo and corporate bonds

## Core idea
India's government bond repo market is deep, but repo in corporate bonds (borrowing cash against corporate bonds as collateral) was tiny, partly because lenders feared counterparty risk. To fix this, SEBI allowed a **Limited Purpose Clearing Corporation (LPCC)** in 2020 to clear and settle repo trades in corporate debt as a central counterparty. The LPCC that was set up is **AMC Repo Clearing Ltd (ARCL)**, funded by mutual fund asset management companies, and authorised by the RBI. It has its own core settlement guarantee fund (SGF) framework, with contributions from the LPCC, clearing members and debt issuers.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Repo trades.** A borrower sells corporate bonds with an agreement to buy them back later at a higher price; the lender earns the repo rate. In tri-party repo, a third party (the LPCC) manages collateral, valuation and settlement.
2. **Central counterparty.** The LPCC novates trades, becoming counterparty to both sides, collecting margins (haircuts on collateral) and marking to market daily.
3. **Core SGF.** Under SEBI's framework (late 2020), the LPCC runs stress tests to determine the fund's required size; contributions come from the LPCC itself, its clearing members and issuers of the debt securities used; shortfalls must be replenished by the LPCC; and part of the LPCC's profits go into the fund.
4. **Capital.** Mutual fund AMCs contributed capital to set up the LPCC (the working group proposed about ₹150 crore), with SEBI adjusting contribution rules in 2021.
5. **Access.** Since 2023, SEBI rules allow certain entities (for example mutual funds, insurers, NBFCs) to participate directly in tri-party repo through the LPCC without being stock exchange members.
6. **Default.** If a member defaults, the LPCC sells collateral and uses margins, then the Core SGF waterfall.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
A mutual fund borrows ₹100 crore for 7 days against AA-rated corporate bonds with market value ₹120 crore (haircut 16.7%). Repo rate 7% a year.
- Repurchase price = 100 × (1 + 0.07 × 7/365) = ₹100.134 crore.
- If bond prices fall 5% to ₹114 crore, the collateral still covers the loan; if they fall 20%, the LPCC calls for more collateral.
- If the borrower defaults, the LPCC sells the bonds; with a 16.7% haircut and margin calls, losses beyond collateral should be rare, but in stress (illiquid bonds, falling prices) they could reach the Core SGF.

## India specifics
- SEBI approved the LPCC in September 2020, after the 2018 to 2019 credit stress (IL&FS, DHFL) showed how illiquid corporate bond markets were and how mutual funds struggled to raise liquidity.
- ARCL provides clearing and settlement for corporate debt repo trades executed on NSE and BSE, and holds RBI authorisation under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act 2007 for CCP services in corporate debt repo.
- Mutual funds can borrow through repo against their corporate bond holdings to meet redemptions instead of selling bonds at distressed prices.
- Government bond repo is cleared separately through the Clearing Corporation of India Ltd (CCIL).

## Real examples and history
- Debt mutual fund stress in 2018 to 2020 (including Franklin Templeton's closure of six debt schemes in April 2020) highlighted the need for liquidity tools in corporate bonds.
- Corporate bond repo volumes have grown from a very low base but remain small compared with government securities repo.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Collateral quality.** Lower-rated bonds need bigger haircuts and may not be eligible.
- **Wrong-way risk.** If the borrower is linked to the bond issuer, the collateral may fall exactly when the borrower defaults.
- **Liquidity of collateral.** In a crisis, selling corporate bonds can be slow and costly, so stress tests must assume longer liquidation periods.
- **Issuer contributions.** Requiring issuers to contribute to the SGF is unusual; it spreads costs but can discourage some issuers.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Treasury and fund managers use corporate bond repo for short-term liquidity.
- Risk managers model haircuts and margin calls in stress scenarios.
- Analysts watch repo market development as a sign of corporate bond market depth.

## Related concepts
Repo and tri-party repo; central counterparties; Core SGF; haircuts; mutual fund liquidity risk; CCIL; corporate bond market.
