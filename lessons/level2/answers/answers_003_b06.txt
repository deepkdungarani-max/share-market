# Indian broker defaults: from the 1992 scam and Ketan Parekh to NSEL and Karvy

## Core idea
India's major market crises share a pattern. Money or securities belonging to others ended up in the hands of brokers or intermediaries. Long or flexible settlement cycles let losses stay hidden. And the link between payments and securities was weak. Each crisis produced reforms aimed at one of these weaknesses, and today's rules (T+1, daily mark-to-market, margin pledges, upstreaming, UPI block) are best read as the sum of those lessons.

## Timeline of major episodes
1. **1991 to 1992: the securities scam (Harshad Mehta).** Brokers used bank ready-forward deals and bank receipts to divert roughly ₹5,000 crore of bank funds into the stock market between April 1991 and May 1992, fuelling a bubble that collapsed when the scheme was exposed. Several finance companies, including Fairgrowth Financial Services, appear in accounts of the period. Results: SEBI received statutory powers (SEBI Act, 1992), a special court and custodian were set up to recover assets, and NSE was created with screen-based trading.
2. **2001: Ketan Parekh and the Calcutta Stock Exchange crisis.** Parekh's favoured "K-10" stocks were funded partly with money from cooperative banks. When bear traders hammered them in February 2001, prices collapsed, and on 9 March 2001 brokers, including Parekh, defaulted on payments at the Calcutta Stock Exchange. Results: the carry-forward system (badla) was ended, compulsory rolling settlement began in July 2001, and India moved to T+3 (2002) and then T+2 (2003).
3. **2013: NSEL.** The National Spot Exchange offered "paired contracts" (buy now, sell later at a fixed price) that worked like fixed-return loans backed by commodity stocks that often did not exist. In July 2013 it stopped paying, leaving about ₹5,600 crore owed to roughly 13,000 investors. Results: commodity derivatives regulation moved from the Forward Markets Commission to SEBI in 2015, and brokers who sold the products faced regulatory action.
4. **2019: Karvy and other brokers.** Karvy pledged clients' shares for its own loans. Smaller brokers such as BMA Wealth Creators showed similar problems. Results: margin pledges, DDPI, upstreaming of client funds, client-level collateral reporting and, later, UPI block trading.

## The recurring weaknesses
- **Custody by intermediaries:** brokers holding client money and securities (1992, 2019).
- **Hidden losses:** long settlement periods and carry-forward let positions grow unchecked (2001).
- **Products dressed as trading:** fixed-return schemes on a trading platform (2013).
- **Concentration and leverage:** a few brokers or stocks dominated (2001).

## Worked example: why settlement speed matters
A broker carries a ₹100 crore long position that falls 2% a day for five days.
- Under weekly settlement and carry-forward, nobody collects losses until the week ends. The loss of about ₹9.6 crore (100 × (1 − 0.98⁵)) appears at once, possibly more than the broker can pay.
- Under daily mark-to-market, the clearing corporation collects about ₹2 crore on day 1. If the broker cannot pay, the position is closed out with a loss limited to roughly one day's move plus margin.
Shorter cycles and daily settlement turn a catastrophic surprise into a small, early default.

## Mapping crises to today's rules
- 1992 led to SEBI's powers and screen-based trading with clearing corporations.
- 2001 led to rolling settlement, the end of badla, and the move to exchange-traded derivatives.
- 2013 led to unified regulation and scrutiny of "assured return" products.
- 2019 led to margin pledges, segregation of client assets and direct settlement through banks.

## Common misconceptions
- "Exchange-traded means guaranteed." NSEL's paired contracts traded on an exchange but were not covered by a clearing corporation guarantee like securities trades.
- "Big names are safe." Karvy was one of India's largest brokers.
- "Defaults are about market crashes." Most were about misuse of other people's money; the crash only exposed it.

## Lessons for investors
- Anything promising fixed returns from "trading" deserves suspicion.
- Keep securities in your own demat account and money in your own bank where possible.
- Concentrated, leveraged market stories (a few stocks, one dominant operator) end badly.
- Read depository and exchange alerts; they exist because past frauds went unnoticed.

## Key takeaways
Indian broker failures came from intermediary custody, slow settlement and products disguised as trading. Each crisis (1992, 2001, 2013, 2019) left a reform: SEBI's powers, rolling settlement, unified commodity regulation, and client asset segregation. Today's structure is designed so that a failing broker holds as little client property as possible and losses surface within a day.

# SEBI's Closing Auction Session: phases, price band, reference price and order transition

## Core idea
Until 2026, the closing price of an NSE stock was the volume-weighted average price (VWAP) of the last 30 minutes of trading. Nobody could trade at that price exactly, and index funds and other investors had no single moment to trade at the close. SEBI's circular of 16 January 2026 introduced a Closing Auction Session (CAS), a call auction that sets the close at one price where the most shares trade. It went live on 3 August 2026 for stocks with derivative contracts. A revision announced in October 2026 reshapes the timings from December 2026. Check the latest exchange circulars, because the rules are still changing.

## Phases in the first version (from 3 August 2026)
1. **3:15 to 3:20 pm: reference price.** Continuous trading in covered stocks ends, and a reference price is calculated from recent continuous-session prices.
2. **3:20 to 3:25 pm: order entry.** Limit and market orders can be entered, modified or cancelled.
3. **3:25 to 3:30 pm: limit-only period.** Only limit orders can be entered, modified or cancelled. The window closes randomly in the last two minutes (between 3:28 and 3:30), so nobody knows the exact final second.
4. **3:30 to 3:35 pm: matching.** The equilibrium price is set and orders are matched.
5. **3:35 to 3:50 pm: transition, then 3:50 to 4:00 pm: post-close session** at the closing price.
Equity derivatives traded until 3:40 pm in this version.

## How the equilibrium price is chosen
The auction picks the price that maximises matched volume. Ties are broken by the smallest order imbalance, then by closeness to the reference price.

## Price band and order transition
- Auction orders must lie within a band around the reference price. Reports cite ±3% for orders carried over from continuous trading; confirm the band in the circular.
- Unexecuted limit orders from the continuous session move into the auction automatically. They can be cancelled but not modified.
- Stop-loss orders, and orders priced outside the band, do not move into the auction.

## The October 2026 revision
- A circular dated 15 October 2026 moves CAS to a separate 10-minute session from 3:30 to 3:40 pm, after continuous trading ends at 3:30, from 7 December 2026.
- Order entry closes randomly between 3:38 and 3:40 pm.
- Changes to how unexecuted orders transition follow from 8 February 2027.
- Derivatives trade until 3:45 pm, with expiry-day differences for index contracts reported inconsistently.
- Expiry settlement no longer uses the auction price (covered in a separate lesson).

## Worked example: finding the auction price
Reference price ₹100. Buy orders: market 200, 500 at ₹101, 300 at ₹100.50, 400 at ₹100. Sell orders: market 100, 300 at ₹99.50, 400 at ₹100, 300 at ₹100.50, 500 at ₹101.
- At ₹99.50: demand 1,400, supply 400, so 400 trade.
- At ₹100: demand 1,400, supply 800, so 800 trade.
- At ₹100.50: demand 1,000, supply 1,100, so 1,000 trade.
- At ₹101: demand 700, supply 1,600, so 700 trade.
The closing price is ₹100.50 with 1,000 shares matched and 100 shares of unfilled sell interest.

## Why random closure and bands matter
In any auction, a large order entered at the last second can move the price with no time for others to respond. Random closure removes the certain "last second". Bands limit how far one order can push the price from recent trading. These controls address manipulation of the close, a classic market abuse.

## Practical effects for traders
- Orders for the close should be entered in the auction windows. Market orders must go in during the first order-entry window.
- Algorithms that target the close now trade into a single auction rather than a 30-minute VWAP.
- Backtests using closing prices should note the change in methodology from August 2026.

## Key takeaways
CAS replaced the 30-minute VWAP close with a call auction for stocks with derivatives from 3 August 2026: reference price, order entry, a limit-only window with random closure, then matching at the volume-maximising price. Bands and transition rules limit manipulation. From 7 December 2026 it becomes a separate 3:30 to 3:40 pm session.

# Expiry settlement price design: closing auction versus window VWAP and manipulation incentives

## Core idea
Cash-settled derivatives pay out against one number at expiry: the final settlement price. Huge positions depend on it, so anyone with a large derivative position has an incentive to move that number. The design question is which price is hardest and most expensive to move: a last trade, an average over a window (VWAP), an auction price, or an opening quotation. India has used a 30-minute VWAP, briefly linked settlement to the new closing auction in 2026, and then returned to VWAP.

## Design options
1. **Last traded price.** Easiest to manipulate: one trade at the right moment sets it.
2. **Window VWAP.** NSE's long-standing method for index derivatives uses each constituent's VWAP over the last 30 minutes of continuous trading. Moving it requires trading heavily across the whole window.
3. **Closing auction price.** Liquidity concentrates in one auction; with random closure and price bands it can be robust, but a single price can still be targeted if the auction is thin.
4. **Special opening quotation.** Some US index options settle on the opening prices of the constituents on expiry day.
5. **Intraday auction.** Some European index derivatives settle on a dedicated midday auction on expiry day.

## India's path
- **Long-standing rule:** index derivatives settle on the index calculated from constituents' VWAP in the last 30 minutes (3:00 to 3:30 pm). Stock derivatives settle on the stock's closing price, which was itself the last-30-minute VWAP.
- **August 2026:** the closing auction (CAS) began setting closing prices for stocks with derivatives, and so fed into expiry settlement.
- **October 2026 revision (effective 7 December 2026):** settlement was decoupled from the auction. Index derivatives keep the constituent VWAP from 3:00 to 3:30 pm. Expiring stock derivatives settle at the stock's VWAP across exchanges over the last 30 minutes of continuous trading.

## The economics of manipulation
A manipulator compares two things:
- **Gain:** derivative exposure × move in the settlement price.
- **Cost:** impact cost of the trades needed to move the price, plus the risk of holding those trades.
If derivative exposure is far larger than the underlying trading needed, manipulation can pay even after costs. That is why expiry-day option volumes many times larger than cash volumes are a concern.

## Worked example
A trader has options exposure equivalent to ₹5,000 crore of the index at expiry.
- Moving the settlement price down by 0.2% earns 0.2% × ₹5,000 crore = ₹10 crore.
- To push a 30-minute VWAP down 0.2%, suppose they must sell ₹1,000 crore across the window, paying about 0.3% impact = ₹3 crore, and must later buy back or carry the position.
- Net expected gain is about ₹7 crore before risk: the incentive exists. Window VWAP makes this costlier than targeting one last trade, but not impossible.

## Real cases
SEBI's interim order of 3 July 2025 against Jane Street group entities alleged a pattern on Bank Nifty expiry days: buying constituent stocks and futures in the morning to lift the index while building large option positions that gained when the index later fell, and aggressive selling later in the day. SEBI required a deposit of about ₹4,843 crore into escrow. Jane Street disputed the findings. Whatever the final outcome, the case shows how settlement design and cash-market depth interact with option positions.

## Choosing between auction and VWAP
- VWAP spreads the price over time, so manipulation needs sustained trading; but it is not a tradable price, so hedgers cannot lock in exactly.
- An auction gives a tradable single price and concentrates liquidity; but its robustness depends on depth, bands and random closure.
- Multi-exchange VWAP reduces the effect of trading on one venue.
- Surveillance and position limits are needed whatever the method.

## Key takeaways
The final settlement price should be expensive to move relative to the derivative exposure riding on it. India uses a 30-minute VWAP for index derivatives and, from December 2026, a multi-exchange 30-minute VWAP for stock derivatives, after briefly linking stock settlement to the new closing auction. Manipulation incentives scale with the ratio of derivative exposure to cash-market depth.

# Spoofing, layering and marking the close: SEBI's PFUTP regulations and enforcement

## Core idea
Market manipulation in India is prohibited mainly by section 12A of the SEBI Act and the SEBI (Prohibition of Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices relating to Securities Market) Regulations, 2003, known as PFUTP. The regulations do not name "spoofing" or "layering". SEBI treats them as conduct that creates a false or misleading appearance of trading, prohibited by the general clauses in regulations 3 and 4, especially regulation 4(2)(a). Marking the close, wash trades and synchronised trades are handled the same way.

## The main forms of abuse
1. **Spoofing:** placing large orders you do not intend to execute, to create false demand or supply, trading on the other side, then cancelling.
2. **Layering:** a form of spoofing with many orders at several price levels.
3. **Marking the close:** trading at or near the close to move the closing or settlement price, often to benefit derivative positions or portfolio valuations.
4. **Wash and synchronised trades:** trades without a real change of ownership, or prearranged trades between related parties, to create volume or transfer profits.

## Legal framework
- **SEBI Act section 12A:** prohibits manipulative and deceptive devices.
- **PFUTP regulation 3:** prohibits fraudulent dealings.
- **Regulation 4(1):** prohibits manipulative, fraudulent or unfair trade practices.
- **Regulation 4(2):** lists examples, with clause (a) covering acts that create a false or misleading appearance of trading.
- **Penalties:** monetary penalties under section 15HA (up to ₹25 crore or three times the profit, whichever is higher), disgorgement of gains, market bans, and criminal prosecution in serious cases.
- **Supreme Court guidance:** in SEBI v. Rakhi Trading (February 2018), the Court upheld that synchronised, reversal trades in derivatives with no genuine purpose were manipulative, even though each trade was on the exchange.

## How SEBI proves spoofing
A single large or cancelled order is not spoofing. SEBI relies on cumulative indicators:
- a high share of orders cancelled relative to trades;
- large orders placed away from the best price and cancelled within seconds;
- executions on the opposite side while the large orders are visible;
- repetition across many episodes and stocks;
- profit linked to the pattern.

## Recent cases
- An interim order against PWAPL found buy-side spoofing in Syrma SGS Technology over seven episodes, with profit of about ₹4.44 lakh.
- SEBI has reported larger spoofing investigations across many stocks, with alleged gains in crores.
- SEBI's July 2025 interim order against Jane Street entities alleged expiry-day index manipulation, including "marking the close"-type selling into the last part of the session; the firm disputed it.

## Worked example: a spoofing cycle
A trader holds 5,000 shares to sell at ₹100.05 while the best bid is ₹99.95.
1. They place buy orders for 50,000 shares at ₹99.90, ₹99.85 and ₹99.80 (layering), making demand look strong.
2. Other traders lift their bids; the trader sells 5,000 shares at ₹100.05.
3. Within seconds the 50,000-share buy orders are cancelled, unfilled.
The cycle gains about ₹0.10 per share, ₹500, but repeated hundreds of times it adds up. Surveillance sees a cancellation ratio above 90% on the buy side and sales during each layering episode.

## Compliance points for algo traders
- Every order should have a genuine intention to trade at its price.
- Test strategies for patterns that resemble layering (frequent large orders cancelled after opposite-side fills).
- Keep logs of strategy logic and order decisions; they are evidence of intent.
- Watch order-to-trade ratios; exchanges charge for very high ratios.

## Key takeaways
Spoofing, layering and marking the close are prohibited in India through section 12A and PFUTP regulations 3 and 4, mainly 4(2)(a). SEBI proves them from patterns, not single orders, and can impose penalties up to three times profits, disgorgement and bans. Algo traders should design and document strategies so every order is genuine.

# Frequent batch auctions and market design theory (Budish, Cramton and Shim)

## Core idea
Budish, Cramton and Shim (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2015) argue that the continuous limit order book has a design flaw. Because time is continuous and messages are processed one by one, tiny speed advantages earn real money. When public information moves the price, fast traders race to pick off stale quotes before liquidity providers can cancel them. Liquidity providers widen spreads to pay for these losses, and firms spend heavily on speed. Their proposal: replace continuous trading with frequent batch auctions, uniform-price sealed-bid auctions held at short discrete intervals (for example every tenth of a second).

## The evidence
- Using millisecond exchange data, the authors show that the S&P 500 futures (ES) and the SPY exchange-traded fund, which track the same index, are almost perfectly correlated over minutes but nearly uncorrelated at very short horizons. In their 2011 data the median correlation at the shortest horizons was about 0.09.
- These breakdowns create purely mechanical arbitrage opportunities that go to the fastest trader.
- The opportunities did not shrink as speed rose; the race just got faster.

## The model, step by step
1. A public signal (for example a futures move) makes a quote stale.
2. The liquidity provider sends a cancel; N fast traders send orders to hit the stale quote.
3. Messages are processed in order. With N symmetric racers, the provider wins only about 1 in N+1 races.
4. Losses to sniping are a tax on liquidity provision, paid through wider spreads by investors.
5. Investing in speed only reallocates who wins; it does not remove the tax. It is a socially wasteful arms race.

## Why batching helps
In a batch auction, all orders arriving in the interval are treated as simultaneous and matched at one price. A provider's cancel and a sniper's order in the same batch arrive together, so sniping succeeds only if the signal arrives in the last few microseconds of the interval. Competition shifts from speed to price.

## Worked example
Suppose quotes become stale 10 times a minute, by ₹0.10 each, and investors trade 20 times a minute. With 4 fast snipers, the provider loses each race with probability about 4/5.
- Continuous market: expected sniping loss = 10 × ₹0.10 × 0.8 = ₹0.80 a minute. To break even, the half-spread on 20 investor trades must be at least ₹0.80 ÷ 20 = ₹0.04.
- Batch every 100 milliseconds, with a 1 millisecond latency gap: only jumps in the last 1% of each interval can be sniped, so losses fall by about 99% and the needed half-spread falls to well under ₹0.01.

## Real-world experience
- Call auctions already exist at the open and close (India's pre-open session and, from 2026, the closing auction).
- Taiwan's exchange used periodic call auctions every few seconds for decades before moving to continuous trading in 2020.
- European venues offer periodic auction books, and IEX in the US uses a speed bump to blunt latency arbitrage.
- India's experience with co-location disputes and SEBI's 2016 discussion of measures such as randomisation, minimum resting times and batch auctions show the debate's relevance here.
- Later work using London Stock Exchange message data found latency races to be frequent and a meaningful share of trading volume, supporting the model's central claim.

## Criticisms and open questions
- **Choice of interval:** too long hurts price discovery, too short brings back the race.
- **Fragmentation:** if only one venue batches, the race moves to links between venues.
- **Transition costs:** exchanges earn from speed products and data, which reduces their incentive to change.
- **Information:** some argue fast traders improve price discovery, and batching delays it.

## Key takeaways
Continuous trading turns public information into sniping races that tax liquidity providers and fund a speed arms race. Budish, Cramton and Shim show the mechanical arbitrage in ES and SPY data and propose frequent batch auctions, which make time discrete and shift competition from speed to price. Periodic and closing auctions show the idea in practice, while interval choice and cross-venue effects remain debated.
