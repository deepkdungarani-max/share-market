# IFSC tax regime: s.10(4D)/10(4E)/10(4F) reliefs, the unit-level tax holiday, and GAAR limits

## Core idea
India's International Financial Services Centre (IFSC) at GIFT City competes with Singapore, Dubai and Hong Kong for offshore financial business. Its main attraction is tax: a 10-year profit-tax holiday for IFSC units, exemptions for specified income of funds and non-residents, and lower minimum alternate tax. These incentives sit in the Income-tax Act 1961 (sections 10(4D), 10(4E), 10(4F), 80LA, 115JB and others) and were carried into the Income-tax Act 2025 from 1 April 2026 under new section numbers. General anti-avoidance rules still apply to arrangements whose main purpose is tax avoidance.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Unit-level holiday (section 80LA).** An IFSC unit can deduct 100% of eligible profits for any 10 consecutive years out of the first 15, chosen by the unit. (The 2025 Bill version placed this in clause 147.)
2. **Minimum alternate tax.** IFSC units earning solely in convertible foreign exchange pay MAT at 9% (instead of 15%) under the old regime; MAT credit can be carried forward.
3. **Section 10(4D).** Exempts specified income of specified funds in IFSC (for example certain funds whose units are held by non-residents), such as gains on transfer of securities on IFSC exchanges, to the extent attributable to non-resident unit holders.
4. **Section 10(4E).** Exempts income of non-residents from transfer of non-deliverable forward contracts and certain offshore derivatives entered with offshore banking units in IFSC.
5. **Section 10(4F).** Exempts royalty or interest income of non-residents from leasing aircraft (and ships, under related provisions) to IFSC units.
6. **Other reliefs.** Exemptions for non-residents on transfer of certain securities on IFSC exchanges, concessional treatment for relocated funds, and exemptions from some transaction taxes on IFSC trades.
7. **Sunset dates.** Several benefits apply only to units starting operations before a cut-off date; budgets have extended these (reports indicate extension to 2030 in Budget 2025; confirm in the Finance Act).
8. **GAAR.** India's General Anti-Avoidance Rules (applicable since April 2017 to arrangements with tax benefit above ₹3 crore) let tax authorities deny benefits of arrangements whose main purpose is a tax benefit and which lack commercial substance; IFSC status does not exempt sham structures.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
An IFSC fund manager earns ₹50 crore of profit a year.
- Without incentives at 25% corporate tax: ₹12.5 crore tax.
- With section 80LA (100% deduction): normal tax nil; MAT at 9% of book profit: ₹4.5 crore, creditable against future tax after the holiday.
Over 10 years, savings versus 25% tax: about ₹80 crore (₹125 crore normal tax minus ₹45 crore MAT), before MAT credit use.

A non-resident investor in an IFSC fund earns a $1 million gain on securities traded on an IFSC exchange: exempt under section 10(4D) to the extent attributable to non-resident unit holders, versus taxable at Indian rates if earned onshore.

## India specifics
- The Income-tax Act 2025 (in force from 1 April 2026) renumbered these provisions; the IFSC deduction appears in the 2025 Act's equivalent of section 80LA.
- IFSC units also benefit from GST and stamp duty concessions and from operating in foreign currency.
- The IFSCA regulates entities; tax benefits depend on meeting both IFSCA and tax conditions.

## Real examples and history
- Aircraft leasing in GIFT City grew rapidly after section 10(4F) and other exemptions, with lessors moving from Ireland and Singapore for India-related leases.
- Fund management and banking units (IBUs) form the largest share of GIFT City activity.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Ten consecutive years.** The holiday must be continuous within the 15-year window; skipping years is not allowed.
- **Only eligible income.** Income from non-IFSC operations is not covered.
- **MAT still applies** under the old regime, though at a lower rate.
- **Substance.** Entities need real operations (staff, offices) in IFSC; shell structures risk GAAR and other challenges.
- **Section numbers changed in 2026.** Use the 2025 Act's numbering for tax year 2026-27 onward.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Fund managers compare IFSC and overseas domiciles for after-tax returns.
- Foreign investors consider IFSC funds and exchanges for India exposure with tax efficiency.
- Tax advisers structure IFSC operations with substance to withstand GAAR scrutiny.

## Related concepts
IFSCA regulations; GIFT Nifty; LRS and resident access to IFSC; GAAR; Income-tax Act 2025; MAT.

# LRS, overseas portfolio investment rules and resident access to IFSC exchanges

## Core idea
Resident Indians can invest abroad, but within limits. The RBI's **Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS)** allows each resident individual to remit up to US$250,000 per financial year for permitted purposes, including investing in foreign shares and funds. The **Overseas Investment Rules and Regulations of 2022** under FEMA define what residents can buy (overseas portfolio investment, OPI). Remittances above a threshold attract **tax collected at source (TCS)**. Residents can also use LRS to invest through IFSC (GIFT City) platforms in US dollars.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Limit.** US$250,000 per person per financial year across all LRS purposes (travel, education, gifts, investment).
2. **Process.** The investor remits through an authorised bank, declaring the purpose; the bank reports it.
3. **Permitted investments (OPI).** Listed foreign shares, foreign mutual funds and ETFs, units of investment funds, and other permitted securities; unlisted shares only within specific conditions.
4. **TCS.** Banks collect TCS on LRS remittances above ₹10 lakh per financial year (threshold raised from ₹7 lakh from 1 April 2025); for investments the rate above the threshold is 20%. TCS is not a final tax: it is credited against the investor's income tax or refunded.
5. **IFSC access.** Residents can open accounts with IFSC brokers and trade permitted products on IFSC exchanges (for example US stocks through IFSC platforms), funded under LRS in US dollars.
6. **Reporting and tax.** Foreign assets must be disclosed in the income tax return (Schedule FA), and foreign income and gains are taxed in India, with credit for foreign taxes under treaties where applicable.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
An investor remits ₹25 lakh in FY 2026-27 to buy US stocks:
- TCS-free threshold: ₹10 lakh.
- TCS on the excess ₹15 lakh at 20% = ₹3 lakh collected by the bank (shown in the investor's tax credit statement).
- If the investor's final tax liability for the year is ₹5 lakh, the ₹3 lakh TCS is adjusted against it; if liability is lower, the excess is refunded after filing the return.
LRS check: ₹25 lakh at ₹85 per US$ ≈ $29,400, well within $250,000.

## India specifics
- LRS limits have risen over time (from $25,000 in 2004 to $250,000 since 2015).
- The 2022 overseas investment framework replaced older FEMA regulations, clarifying OPI versus overseas direct investment (ODI).
- Budget 2025 removed TCS on education remittances funded by loans and raised the general threshold to ₹10 lakh; later changes (for example reported reductions for education and medical remittances in 2026) should be checked in the current Finance Act.
- Resident investment in IFSC is under LRS, with IFSC entities treated as foreign for FEMA purposes.

## Real examples and history
- Interest in US stocks among Indian retail investors grew after 2020 through app-based platforms, many routing through GIFT City or overseas brokers.
- In 2022, SEBI asked mutual funds to pause overseas investments when the industry-wide limit ($7 billion) was reached, pushing some investors to direct LRS routes.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **TCS is not an extra tax.** It is creditable, though it ties up cash until adjusted.
- **Per person limit.** Family members each have their own $250,000 limit.
- **Unspent funds.** Money remitted for investment should be invested within RBI timelines or repatriated.
- **Schedule FA.** Failing to disclose foreign assets can attract heavy penalties under the Black Money Act.
- **Currency risk.** Returns in rupees depend on USD/INR.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Investors diversify internationally through LRS, accounting for TCS cash flow and taxes.
- Platforms offering US stocks structure accounts through IFSC or foreign brokers within LRS.
- Financial planners track each family member's LRS usage.

## Related concepts
FEMA; IFSC; GIFT Nifty; TCS and income tax credits; Schedule FA; currency risk.

# GIFT Nifty basis, quanto adjustment and overnight gap forecasting

## Core idea
GIFT Nifty futures trade in GIFT City in US dollars for about 21 hours a day, including hours when NSE is closed. Traders watch GIFT Nifty to predict where Nifty will open. But GIFT Nifty's price differs from the Nifty index for three reasons: the normal cost of carry (futures versus spot), a **quanto adjustment** (the contract pays in dollars on a rupee index, with a fixed dollar multiplier), and news arriving while NSE is shut. Separating these gives a cleaner forecast of the next day's gap.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Contract.** GIFT Nifty futures settle in USD with a fixed dollar value per index point, so gains and losses depend on the Nifty level in points, not on USD/INR: a quanto contract.
2. **Fair value (carry).** Domestic futures fair value F = S × e^((r − q)T), where r is the rupee interest rate and q the dividend yield.
3. **Quanto adjustment.** Because payoffs are in dollars, the fair value is adjusted by the covariance between Nifty and the rupee's dollar value: F_quanto ≈ F × e^(−ρ σ_S σ_X T), where ρ is the correlation between Nifty and the rupee's USD value (often positive: the rupee tends to weaken when Indian stocks fall), σ_S Nifty's volatility, σ_X the rupee's volatility. With positive ρ, quanto fair value is slightly below domestic fair value.
4. **Basis at NSE close.** At 3:30 pm, GIFT Nifty minus Nifty ≈ carry plus quanto adjustment (plus noise).
5. **Overnight change.** Movement in GIFT Nifty after NSE closes reflects global news (US markets, Asian open).
6. **Forecasting the open.** Expected Nifty open ≈ Nifty close × (GIFT Nifty at 9:00 am / GIFT Nifty at 3:30 pm previous day), adjusted for any change in carry; or regress the actual opening gap on the GIFT Nifty change.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
Nifty closes at 25,000. Rupee interest rate 6.5%, dividend yield 1.3%, near-month contract with 20 days to expiry (T = 20/365 = 0.0548).
- Carry = 25,000 × (e^(0.052 × 0.0548) − 1) ≈ 25,000 × 0.00285 ≈ 71 points.
- Quanto adjustment with ρ = 0.4, σ_S = 14%, σ_X = 5%: 0.4 × 0.14 × 0.05 × 0.0548 = 0.000153 → about 4 points lower.
- Fair GIFT Nifty at NSE close ≈ 25,000 + 71 − 4 = 25,067.
Overnight GIFT Nifty moves from 25,067 to 25,280 by 9:00 am (+0.85%). Forecast Nifty open ≈ 25,000 × 1.0085 ≈ 25,212.
If historically the slope of opening gaps on GIFT Nifty changes is 0.95, the forecast is 25,000 × (1 + 0.95 × 0.0085) ≈ 25,202.

## India specifics
- GIFT Nifty replaced SGX Nifty after trading migrated to NSE International Exchange in July 2023; it trades in two sessions covering Asian, European and US hours.
- Domestic Nifty futures and GIFT Nifty can show different bases because of rupee versus dollar interest rates, quanto effects and different participants.
- The pre-open session (9:00 to 9:15 am) sets Nifty's opening price, often close to GIFT Nifty-implied levels.

## Real examples and history
- On days with major US moves overnight, GIFT Nifty's move has typically been a good guide to the Nifty open, though Indian-specific news after the GIFT session and pre-open order imbalances cause deviations.
- Quanto adjustments are standard in pricing products like Nikkei futures traded in dollars on the CME.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Comparing GIFT Nifty with spot directly.** Remove the carry and quanto basis first.
- **Contract rolls.** Near-month and next-month GIFT contracts have different carry.
- **Indian news after GIFT trading.** Policy announcements before 9:00 am may not be in GIFT Nifty yet.
- **Thin liquidity in late sessions.** GIFT Nifty quotes at odd hours may be less reliable.
- **Overestimating quanto.** It is usually small for short maturities.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Day traders use GIFT Nifty to plan opening trades and hedges.
- Quants build gap forecasts from GIFT Nifty, US index moves and currency moves, testing out of sample.
- Foreign investors hedge Indian equity exposure in dollars through GIFT Nifty.

## Related concepts
Futures fair value; quanto derivatives; overnight gap risk; pre-open session; NSE IFSC Clearing; LRS.

# IFSCA regulations for funds, GCMUs, family investment funds and capital market intermediaries

## Core idea
The International Financial Services Centres Authority (IFSCA) regulates all financial activity in GIFT City's IFSC. For fund management and capital markets, its key frameworks are the **Fund Management Regulations 2025** (for fund management entities and their schemes, including family investment funds), the **Capital Market Intermediaries Regulations 2025** (for brokers, research entities and others), and frameworks for global in-house centres of financial groups. These aim to make GIFT City a competitive base for funds and intermediaries serving global and Indian clients in foreign currency.

## How it works, step by step
**Fund Management Regulations 2025 (in force February 2025, replacing the 2022 regulations):**
1. **Fund management entities (FMEs)** must register with IFSCA before operating, meeting net worth, key personnel (principal officer, compliance officer with at least 5 years' relevant experience) and governance requirements.
2. **Scheme types:** venture capital schemes, restricted schemes (for accredited or high-value investors, similar to alternative investment funds), retail schemes (open to wider investors with stricter rules), plus ETFs and other structures.
3. **Minimum corpus.** For venture capital and restricted schemes, reduced from US$5 million to US$3 million in April 2025; open-ended schemes can start investing with US$1 million and reach the minimum within 12 months.
4. **Family investment funds (FIFs).** Self-managed funds pooling money of a single family, with a high minimum corpus, allowing family offices to invest globally from IFSC.
5. **Custody, valuation, disclosure** rules, with amendments in December 2025 including a migration window for appointing IFSC-based custodians.

**Capital Market Intermediaries Regulations 2025 (April 2025):** register broker-dealers, clearing members, depository participants, investment advisers and a new category of research entities, with capital and conduct rules.

**Global in-house centres (GICs).** Financial groups can set up IFSC units providing services to group entities; IFSCA approved new GIC rules in December 2025. The topic list refers to "GCMUs"; in IFSCA's published frameworks the closest category is these global in-house centres, so check the current IFSCA regulation for the exact term.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
A manager launches a restricted scheme in GIFT City:
- Minimum corpus: US$3 million; it raises $20 million from 25 investors (minimum investment per investor as specified, for example $150,000 for accredited investors).
- Management fee 1.5% = $300,000 a year; IFSC unit profits eligible for the 10-year tax holiday.
- Non-resident investors' income from specified securities transactions may be exempt under IFSC tax provisions.
A family investment fund for one family with $50 million can invest in global equities, private funds and real estate from IFSC under LRS for resident family members (subject to limits).

## India specifics
- GIFT City funds can also relocate from overseas jurisdictions with tax-neutral relocation provisions.
- IFSC schemes operate in foreign currency; Indian residents invest through LRS within limits.
- IFSCA has progressively eased rules to attract managers (lower corpus, faster launches) while strengthening governance.

## Real examples and history
- Fund management is one of the fastest-growing segments in GIFT City since 2020, along with banking and aircraft leasing.
- The 2022 regulations created the first comprehensive framework; the 2025 rewrite simplified and updated it.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Transition.** Schemes filed under old rules have specific transition paths.
- **Investor eligibility.** Restricted schemes are for accredited or high-value investors only.
- **Resident investors.** LRS limits apply to Indian residents.
- **Terminology.** Regulations change names and categories; use current IFSCA texts.
- **Substance.** Tax benefits need genuine IFSC operations.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Fund managers choose IFSC for India-focused funds with foreign investors, or global funds for Indian investors.
- Wealth managers use FIFs for family offices.
- Analysts track GIFT City's growth in assets under management and registrations.

## Related concepts
IFSC tax regime; LRS; NSE IFSC Clearing; GIFT Nifty; AIF regulations; family offices.

# NSE Clearing internal shortage auctions: rules, fees and broker close-out policies

## Core idea
When a seller fails to deliver shares on settlement day, the buyer must still be protected. NSE Clearing handles such **short deliveries** by running a buying-in **auction** on the settlement day, buying shares from other sellers at the defaulter's cost. If no shares are found, the trade is **closed out** at a punitive price. Shortages can also be **internal**, when a broker's own selling client fails to deliver to its own buying client; since late 2025 these are also closed out at the clearing member level under NSE Clearing's process.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Short delivery.** A seller sells shares it does not deliver (for example selling shares not yet received, or BTST sales where the purchase failed).
2. **Auction (T+1).** After pay-out on the settlement day, NSE Clearing conducts a buying-in auction in the trading system for the short quantity.
3. **Auction price.** Sellers bid in the auction; the defaulting seller pays the auction price; if it exceeds the original sale price, the seller bears the difference.
4. **Close-out if auction fails.** If no shares are bought in the auction, the trade is closed out in cash at the **higher of** (a) the highest price on the exchange from the trade day to the auction day, or (b) 20% above the official closing price on the auction day. The buyer receives this amount instead of shares.
5. **Internal shortages.** When the buying and selling clients belong to the same broker, the broker historically handled them through internal auctions or close-outs under its policy. NSE Clearing's November 2025 framework has the CC execute close-out for both CC and internal shortages at the clearing member level, which members pass to the short-delivering and receiving clients.
6. **Corporate actions.** If shortages span a record date, entitlements are handled separately (for example cash compensation for dividends).

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
A client sells 500 shares at ₹400 but fails to deliver.
- **Auction succeeds** at ₹412: the defaulter pays 500 × (412 − 400) = ₹6,000 extra plus charges.
- **Auction fails:** the highest price from trade day to auction day was ₹430; the auction-day close is ₹405, so 20% above is ₹486. Close-out price = max(430, 486) = ₹486. The defaulter pays 500 × (486 − 400) = ₹43,000 to the buyer (beyond the sale proceeds it doesn't receive), a heavy penalty.
- The buyer gets ₹486 × 500 = ₹2.43 lakh instead of shares, a windfall relative to the ₹400 purchase if the price is ₹405.

## India specifics
- Shortages often arise from BTST (buy today, sell tomorrow) trades when the purchase leg fails to deliver, from corporate actions, or from errors.
- Under T+1, the auction occurs on the settlement day itself, after pay-out.
- Brokers publish internal shortage policies, often matching the exchange's close-out rate.

## Real examples and history
- Shortages in stocks with sharp price rises (especially small caps or stocks in upper circuit) can lead to very large close-out costs for short sellers who could not deliver.
- The 20% markup has existed for years to deter deliberate short delivery.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Selling shares in transit.** Selling shares bought the previous day can fail if the earlier seller defaults (BTST risk).
- **Pledged or frozen shares.** Shares that are pledged or blocked cannot be delivered, causing shortage.
- **Upper circuit stocks.** No sellers in the auction leads to close-out at punitive rates.
- **Buyers' surprise.** Buyers may receive cash instead of shares and miss gains above the close-out price.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Traders avoid selling shares they do not have free and clear; brokers block sales of unsettled holdings in risky cases.
- Buyers of BTST shares understand the risk that their own buy may fail.
- Event-driven traders note that close-out rules make naked short selling in cash markets costly.
