# IPF administration: corpus sources, trust structure, claim process and cap history at NSE and BSE

## Core idea
When a stock broker defaults and cannot return clients' money or securities, investors can claim compensation from the exchange's **Investor Protection Fund (IPF)**, held in a trust separate from the exchange. The IPF pays only the shortfall that remains after the defaulter's own assets are used, and only up to a cap per investor per claim. NSE raised its cap from ₹25 lakh to ₹35 lakh for defaults declared after 13 August 2024.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Trust structure.** Each exchange sets up an IPF Trust with trustees (including public interest directors and others), keeping the fund separate from the exchange's own money.
2. **Corpus sources.** Contributions from the exchange (as specified by SEBI, such as a share of listing fees and penalties or fines collected from members), interest earned on the corpus, and other amounts specified in SEBI's framework. SEBI has periodically asked exchanges to review and enlarge corpus sizes.
3. **Default declaration.** The exchange declares a broker a defaulter (or expels it) after it fails to meet obligations.
4. **Public notice and claims.** The exchange publishes a notice inviting claims within a set period (typically up to three years from the notice).
5. **Assessment.** A committee checks each claim: whether the transaction was on the exchange, whether the claim is genuine and within limits, and how much the defaulter's assets (the defaulters' account) can pay.
6. **Payment.** The IPF pays the shortfall between the admitted claim and what the defaulter's assets provide, up to the per-investor cap.
7. **Beyond the cap.** Investors with larger claims must pursue the balance through arbitration or civil suits against the defaulter.
8. **Review.** NSE's byelaws require the exchange, with the IPF Trust, to review and progressively increase the per-claim limit every three years.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
A broker defaults after August 2024. An investor's admitted claim is ₹48 lakh (funds and securities owed). The defaulter's assets cover 30% of admitted claims.
- From the defaulters' account: ₹48 lakh × 30% = ₹14.4 lakh.
- Shortfall: ₹33.6 lakh. The IPF pays up to ₹35 lakh per investor per claim; total compensation is limited to the shortfall, so the IPF pays ₹33.6 lakh. The investor recovers the full ₹48 lakh in this case.
- If the admitted claim were ₹80 lakh, 30% recovery = ₹24 lakh; shortfall ₹56 lakh; IPF pays ₹35 lakh; the investor recovers ₹59 lakh and must pursue ₹21 lakh separately.

## India specifics
- NSE's limit was ₹25 lakh before the August 2024 increase to ₹35 lakh; BSE has its own limits, historically lower (older reports cite ₹15 lakh), so check BSE's current IPF page.
- SEBI asked NSE in 2020 to increase its IPF corpus substantially (to about ₹1,500 crore) after a review, reflecting larger trading volumes and recent broker defaults.
- IPF covers investors' claims arising from trades on the exchange; it does not compensate for market losses or for losses from off-exchange schemes run by brokers.

## Real examples and history
- Broker defaults such as Karvy Stock Broking (2019 to 2020) and several smaller firms led to IPF claim processes; many clients' securities were recovered directly via depositories, reducing IPF payouts.
- Earlier defaults (for example Ficus Securities) raised questions about responsibility and the adequacy of IPF coverage.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Not a guarantee of all losses.** The IPF pays only admitted claims for exchange-related obligations, up to the cap.
- **Time limits.** Claims must be filed within the notice period.
- **Off-market deals.** Losses from "assured return" schemes or private arrangements with brokers are usually not covered.
- **Funds versus securities.** Securities held in the client's own demat account are generally safe from broker default; idle funds with brokers are more exposed, though running-account settlement rules reduce them.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Investors keep minimal idle funds with brokers and rely on demat holdings in their own name.
- Investors in failed brokers file claims quickly and document transactions.
- Analysts of exchanges note IPF corpus sizes and contributions as part of investor protection.

## Related concepts
Broker default procedures; running-account settlement; margin pledge; Karvy case; clearing corporation guarantees; arbitration.

# Insolvency of a stock broker: trust principle, IBC and client claims

## Core idea
When a stock broker fails, the key question for clients is whether their money and shares are part of the broker's estate (shared with all creditors) or belong to them. Indian rules require brokers to segregate client funds and securities, treating them as held for clients. Courts and tribunals have also held that stock brokers are "financial service providers" outside the ordinary corporate insolvency process of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC). So broker failures are handled mainly through exchange default procedures, SEBI orders and other legal routes, not a standard IBC resolution.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Segregation.** SEBI rules require client funds to be kept in client bank accounts and client securities in client demat accounts (with pledges only for the client's own margin), separate from the broker's own assets.
2. **Trust principle.** Client assets are held for the clients' benefit; the broker has no right to use them for its own debts. In the Karvy case, SEBI and the depository returned clients' securities that the broker had pledged for its own loans.
3. **Exchange default process.** When a broker fails to meet obligations, the exchange declares it a defaulter, takes over its assets with the exchange (deposits, margins), settles obligations to the clearing corporation, and invites client claims, paying from the defaulter's assets and then the Investor Protection Fund.
4. **IBC.** Section 3(16) and (17) of the IBC treat entities providing financial services under a financial sector regulator's authorisation as financial service providers (FSPs), excluded from the corporate insolvency process unless notified under section 227 (which has so far covered certain NBFCs and housing finance companies). The NCLAT and NCLT benches have held that CIRP applications against stock brokers are not maintainable.
5. **Other routes.** Winding up under the Companies Act, civil suits, criminal cases for misappropriation, and SEBI enforcement (bans, disgorgement).

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
A broker fails with:
- Client securities properly held in clients' own demat accounts: ₹800 crore → unaffected; clients keep them.
- Client funds: ₹120 crore owed to clients, but only ₹70 crore in client bank accounts (₹50 crore misused).
- Broker's own assets: ₹40 crore of deposits with the exchange.
Clients' claims: the ₹70 crore in client accounts is returned; the exchange uses the defaulter's ₹40 crore to pay admitted claims; the remaining ₹10 crore shortfall is paid from the IPF up to the per-investor cap. Clients with claims above the cap pursue the rest legally.

## India specifics
- BMA Wealth Creators (2019) was another case where the exchange compared monthly client fund and security balances reported by the broker with depository and bank records, uncovering misuse.
- After the 2019 failures, SEBI ended pledging of client securities for brokers' borrowing, introduced margin pledge (2020), required running-account settlement of client funds, and moved toward upstreaming client funds to clearing corporations so that brokers do not hold large client balances overnight.
- The FSP insolvency rules of 2019 apply only to FSPs notified by the government; brokers have not been notified.

## Real examples and history
- Karvy Stock Broking (2019 to 2020): client securities were returned, lenders lost, and the broker was expelled and banned.
- Globally, broker failures such as MF Global (2011) and Lehman Brothers' brokerage (2008) showed how client asset segregation and special resolution regimes decide clients' outcomes.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Assuming IBC protects clients.** IBC proceedings against brokers have been held not maintainable; clients rely on segregation and exchange processes.
- **Pooled accounts.** Securities in broker pool or beneficiary accounts are more vulnerable than those in clients' own accounts.
- **Unpaid securities.** Shares bought but not fully paid for may be retained by the broker under rules.
- **Group company exposure.** Brokers with affiliated NBFCs or wealth businesses may move funds between entities.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Investors prefer brokers with strong financials and clean regulatory records, keep holdings in their own demat accounts, and avoid large idle balances.
- Investors monitor depository statements and alerts for unauthorised transfers or pledges.
- Lenders verify that pledged securities are the broker's own, not clients'.

## Related concepts
Segregation of client assets; Karvy case; Investor Protection Fund; margin pledge; IBC and financial service providers; running-account settlement.

# SCORES 2.0 and Smart ODR: conciliation, arbitration, appeals and statistics

## Core idea
Investors with complaints against listed companies, brokers, mutual funds or other SEBI-regulated entities have two main routes. **SCORES** (SEBI Complaints Redress System) is an online complaint platform; its upgraded version, SCORES 2.0, launched in April 2024 with fixed timelines and two levels of review. **Smart ODR** is SEBI's online dispute resolution system, launched in 2023, which takes disputes through online conciliation and, if needed, arbitration, producing binding outcomes.

## How it works, step by step
**SCORES 2.0:**
1. The investor files a complaint online (or through the app), linked to their KYC.
2. The complaint is automatically routed to the entity concerned.
3. The entity must submit an action taken report (ATR) within 21 days.
4. If dissatisfied, the investor can request a first-level review (by the designated body, such as an exchange or depository) within 15 days.
5. If still dissatisfied, a second-level review by SEBI follows, with similar timelines.
6. The investor can instead choose online dispute resolution, closing the SCORES complaint.

**Smart ODR (SEBI's 2023 framework):**
1. The investor (or the entity) first tries to resolve the issue directly with the regulated entity.
2. If unresolved, a dispute is filed on the Smart ODR portal, which assigns it to an ODR institution empanelled by market infrastructure institutions.
3. **Conciliation:** an independent conciliator tries to settle the dispute online within a short, fixed period.
4. **Arbitration:** if conciliation fails, an arbitrator (online) decides, with a binding award within set timelines; fees depend on claim size.
5. Awards can be challenged only on limited grounds under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
An investor complains that a broker failed to transfer ₹1.8 lakh after a sale.
- SCORES: complaint filed on day 0; broker's ATR due by day 21. The investor is unhappy with the ATR on day 23 and requests a first-level review (within 15 days). The designated body reviews within its timeline.
- ODR alternative: the investor files on Smart ODR; conciliation succeeds with the broker agreeing to pay ₹1.8 lakh plus ₹6,000 interest; the case closes without arbitration.

Monthly volumes: SEBI reported resolving about 5,548 SCORES complaints in May 2026 and about 5,037 in June 2026, with pending complaints falling slightly.

## India specifics
- SCORES was first launched in 2011; SCORES 2.0 added auto-routing, KYC-linked registration, shorter timelines and two-level review.
- The ODR framework (2023) replaced exchange-run arbitration as the main route for investor disputes with intermediaries and listed companies, covering a broad range of regulated entities.
- Reported data has shown entities taking a few days on average to submit ATRs, and first-level reviews resolved in about a week, though timings vary by month.

## Real examples and history
- Before ODR, investors relied on exchange arbitration (for broker disputes) and courts (for others), which could be slow and costly.
- SEBI publishes monthly statistics of complaints received and resolved, by entity type.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Not for market losses.** Complaints must concern a service failure or rule breach, not investment losses from price falls.
- **Time limits.** Complaints and disputes must be filed within limitation periods (for example within a set time from the cause of action).
- **Direct approach first.** SCORES and ODR usually require the investor to have approached the entity first.
- **Closure on choosing ODR.** Opting for ODR closes the SCORES complaint.
- **Evidence.** Keep contract notes, statements and correspondence.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Investors use SCORES for service failures (delays in refunds, transfers, dividends) and ODR for disputes needing a binding decision.
- Compliance teams at intermediaries track complaint ageing and ATR timelines.
- Analysts use SEBI's complaint statistics as indicators of service quality across entities.

## Related concepts
Investor grievance redressal; arbitration; regulated intermediaries; LODR Regulation 13 (investor grievances); Investor Protection Fund.

# Exchange enhanced supervision, running account settlement and broker reporting rules

## Core idea
Brokers hold large amounts of client money and act on clients' securities, so regulators check continuously that client assets are safe. SEBI's **enhanced supervision** framework (2016) standardised how brokers segregate, report and are inspected for client funds and securities. **Running-account settlement** forces brokers to return idle client funds periodically, so money does not pile up in broker accounts. Together with later reforms after the 2019 broker failures, these rules reduce the risk of misuse.

## How it works, step by step
**Enhanced supervision (SEBI circular of September 2016, later amended):**
1. Client bank accounts must be named and kept separate from the broker's own accounts; client securities are held in client demat accounts or designated accounts.
2. Brokers report client fund balances and securities to exchanges regularly (monthly, and in other formats as required); exchanges reconcile reports with bank and depository data.
3. Exchanges run risk-based inspections and alert systems; mismatches trigger action.
4. A "net worth" and liquidity check on brokers and restrictions on using client funds.

**Running-account settlement:**
1. A client can choose quarterly or monthly settlement of their running account.
2. On the settlement date, the broker returns funds above what is needed for obligations (margin and pending dues), within limits.
3. Since SEBI's July 2022 circular, settlement dates are the first Friday of the quarter or month (from October 2022); a 2024 change allowed the first Friday or Saturday, with client funds received kept in an upstreaming account so one client's money cannot settle another's.
4. Settlement of securities in running accounts was discontinued in 2019, so the rules cover funds.
5. Exchanges publish the annual calendar of settlement dates.

**Upstreaming.** Brokers must move clients' funds to clearing corporations at the end of each day (with limited exceptions), so client money sits with the CC rather than with brokers overnight.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
A client has ₹5,00,000 with a broker on the quarterly settlement date. Margin required for open F&O positions: ₹2,40,000. Brokers may retain margin needed plus a small buffer (for example up to a specified multiple of the day's requirement, or a fixed amount).
- Retained: ₹2,40,000 (plus allowed buffer, say ₹10,000) = ₹2,50,000.
- Returned to the client's bank account: ₹2,50,000.
A client with no open positions and ₹80,000 idle gets the whole amount back.

## India specifics
- The 2019 failures (Karvy and others) exposed weaknesses: brokers had used client funds and securities for their own purposes. SEBI then banned pledging client securities for broker borrowing, introduced margin pledge (2020), and pushed upstreaming of client funds.
- Clients receive monthly statements, contract notes and depository alerts as additional checks.
- Exchanges can declare brokers defaulters, suspend them, or freeze accounts when reconciliation fails.

## Real examples and history
- BMA Wealth Creators' misuse was flagged when NSE compared reported monthly balances with depository records.
- Before running-account settlement was enforced, many clients left large balances with brokers for years.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Clients forgetting settlement dates.** Funds returned on settlement dates may need to be transferred back for trading.
- **Margin buffer.** Retained amounts depend on positions and rules; check statements.
- **Choice of monthly or quarterly.** More frequent settlement reduces exposure to broker failure.
- **Inactive clients.** Funds of clients with no trades for a period must be returned.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- Investors choose monthly settlement and avoid leaving idle money with brokers.
- Brokers' operations teams plan liquidity for settlement dates.
- Regulators and exchanges use reported data and reconciliations to detect problems early.

## Related concepts
Segregation of client assets; margin pledge; upstreaming; Karvy case; Investor Protection Fund; broker default.

# Market data dissemination: unicast versus multicast, hardware timestamps and equal-length cabling in exchange co-location

## Core idea
In electronic markets, the order in which traders see data and reach the exchange can decide who profits. Exchanges therefore design their data feeds and co-location facilities to give every participant equal access. Key choices are how market data is sent (**unicast**, one copy per recipient, versus **multicast**, one copy delivered to all at once), how precisely events are timed (**hardware timestamps**), and how physical connections are built (**equal-length cabling** so no co-located server is physically closer). India's NSE co-location case showed what goes wrong when these are not designed for fairness.

## How it works, step by step
1. **Unicast (for example TCP).** The exchange sends data separately to each subscriber. Messages queue per connection; some recipients get them earlier, depending on order and server load. TCP also retransmits lost packets, which adds delay.
2. **Multicast (usually UDP).** The exchange sends one stream; network switches replicate it to all subscribers simultaneously. Lost packets are not retransmitted on the main feed; recipients use recovery channels. Delivery is near-simultaneous for all on the same network.
3. **Equal-length cabling.** In co-location halls, cables from each rack to the exchange's switches are cut to the same length, so light travels the same distance to every participant (about 5 nanoseconds per metre in fibre).
4. **Hardware timestamps.** Network cards and switches stamp messages at the hardware level with precise, synchronised clocks, allowing exchanges and regulators to reconstruct event sequences to microseconds or better.
5. **Clock synchronisation rules.** Regulations such as the EU's MiFID II require trading venues and high-frequency traders to synchronise clocks to UTC within tight limits (for example 100 microseconds for HFT) with fine timestamp granularity.
6. **Fair access policies.** Same products and services available to all at the same price; transparent connection rules; audits.

## Formulas and numbers (worked example)
Two co-located servers: A's cable is 20 metres, B's is 50 metres. Light in fibre travels about 2 × 10⁸ m/s: A's delay ≈ 100 ns, B's ≈ 250 ns. A sees every message about 150 ns earlier. In a race to react to a price change, A wins whenever processing times are similar. Equal-length cabling removes this physical advantage.

Unicast queueing: if an exchange server sends each message to 100 subscribers in sequence and each send takes 1 microsecond, the last subscriber receives data about 99 microseconds after the first, an enormous gap for fast traders. Multicast avoids this sequential delay.

## India specifics
- In the NSE co-location case (2015 complaint), NSE's tick-by-tick (TBT) feed was disseminated in a way that let some members connected first, or to less loaded servers, receive data earlier. SEBI found that fair and equitable access had not been designed into the TBT dissemination architecture; NSE was ordered to disgorge about ₹625 crore with interest (2019), and the matter was finally settled in 2025 to 2026 for ₹1,491.21 crore.
- SEBI separately fined NSE ₹1 crore for failing to ensure a level playing field for TBT subscribers.
- In 2016 to 2017 SEBI asked exchanges to give market participants fair access to data and consulted on measures for co-location and algorithmic trading (including ideas such as randomisation of order processing and minimum resting times).

## Real examples and history
- US exchanges adopted equal-length cabling in co-location facilities around 2010 to 2012 after concerns about physical advantages.
- The US SEC has acted against exchanges that sent data to proprietary feeds faster than to the consolidated public feed, highlighting data-feed fairness.

## Edge cases and common mistakes
- **Multicast is not perfectly equal.** Differences in network cards, switches and software still create small gaps.
- **Retransmission.** With UDP, lost packets must be recovered from other channels, which can disadvantage participants with poorer infrastructure.
- **Outside co-location.** Participants outside the facility always face larger delays; fairness rules apply within access categories.
- **Speed bumps.** Deliberate delays can level the field but have trade-offs and remain controversial.

## How traders, investors and quants use it
- HFT firms invest in hardware (FPGAs, kernel-bypass networking) within fair-access rules.
- Regulators use hardware timestamps for surveillance and reconstruction of events.
- Researchers measure latency advantages and their effects on market quality.
